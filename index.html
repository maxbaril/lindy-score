Lindy Durability Scorecard

Citation counts measure attention, not lasting value. This tool scores three bibliometric proxies for durability (sustained citation rate, citation half-life, and cross-specialty diffusion) using data from OpenAlex. Two more dimensions, educational reuse and expert-validated relevance, need a human judgment call, so they're left as free-text fields and kept out of the score.

The composite score below is a provisional heuristic, not a validated instrument, and it omits the two human-judgment dimensions. Papers under 10 years old, or whose citation series hasn't yet peaked and declined, are deliberately left unscored. See how this is computed for why.

Papers

Type a DOI (the https://doi.org/ part is optional) or, for papers old enough to predate DOIs, just the title. We'll search OpenAlex for it.

Peak / half-life smoothing basis

A single unusually high year, often just an indexing quirk rather than a real citation spike, can look like an inflated peak followed by a false "decline" in the raw series. Smoothing feeds both the half-life calculation and the sustained-rate sub-score, so they're built on the same basis. The raw, unsmoothed half-life is always shown next to the active basis on every scorecard, and the outlier flags on each scorecard name the specific years causing trouble.

Sustained-decline requirement for half-life

Half-life is "reached" at the first later year that begins a run of this many consecutive years, all at or below half the smoothed peak, rather than any single year dipping below it just once. A Monte Carlo simulation under negative-binomial citation noise found that the single-year rule flags a spurious half-life on a genuinely flat, non-declining series roughly 71 to 89% of the time. Requiring several consecutive years cuts that substantially without giving up much detection of real declines (95% or better in the same simulation, across every condition tested). See how this is computed for the full comparison. The original single-year result on the raw series is always shown for reference, regardless of this setting.

Rare-field noise threshold

A field survives only if it clears both the minimum citing-works count and the minimum percentage. Fall short on either one and it's dropped. Richness and the entropy-based measures are far more sensitive to this than the top-share measure, so it's worth comparing a scorecard's raw/merged diffusion table with the toggle on and off.

How this is computed, and what it cannot tell you

Data source
Everything comes from the OpenAlex API, queried live in your browser, no login required. The annual citation series comes from every work that cites the paper, grouped by its own publication year: the same approach OpenAlex's own website uses for its "Year" chart. That gives the complete citation history with no cutoff. Field breakdown works the same way, grouped by subfield instead of year.

For anyone checking the numbers: the underlying queries are works?filter=cites:{id}&group_by=publication_year and the equivalent with group_by=primary_topic.subfield.id, both requesting the maximum 200 groups per page. No real paper comes close to that limit, but it's flagged anyway just in case. Citing works with no recorded publication year are counted and shown separately rather than silently dropped.
Two citation totals, shown side by side
OpenAlex also offers a shortcut field with citations pre-counted by year, but it only covers roughly the last decade. An earlier version of this tool used it, which quietly truncated older papers' history and made their "peak year" look suspiciously recent. Every count here now comes from counting citing works directly instead. That live count can end up slightly different from the total OpenAlex has cached on the paper's own record, since the two are simply refreshed on different schedules. Rather than pick a winner, both numbers are shown on the scorecard. Every calculation on this page uses the live, directly-counted total.
Series-start check
If the earliest citing work found is more than five years after the paper's own publication date, the scorecard flags it. That's usually a sign of a gap in OpenAlex's indexing, rather than a paper that genuinely took years to get its first citation. Since the series is now complete history rather than a limited recent window, this is just a sanity check, not a warning about missing data.
Citation persistence
The sum of citations over the last five complete calendar years; the current year, still in progress, is left out. "Still rising" means the most recent complete year is at least as high as every other year in the series.
Citation half-life
Count how many years pass between the peak citation year and the first later year where the rate drops to half the peak or lower (see "sustained-decline requirement" below for what "drops" actually requires). If that never happens, the tool says so plainly, "not reached after N years," rather than making up a number.

A single unusually high year, often an indexing quirk rather than a real spike in attention, can throw this off badly. Salter and Harris's 1963 paper, for example, shows 17 citations in 1999, 79 in 2000, and 31 in 2001. Treating 79 as the real peak overstates it roughly threefold against the neighbouring years' 24-citation average. So by default, peak and half-life are worked out on a 3-year centered moving average instead of the raw numbers: each year's smoothed value is the average of itself and its immediate neighbours, using whatever years are available at the start or end of the series rather than dropping them. The smoothed peak, not the raw one, is also what the sustained-rate sub-score below is divided by, so both metrics are built on the same footing. You can switch to raw numbers or a 5-year average using the setting above, but the original single-year result on the raw series is always shown next to whichever basis is active. It's never hidden.
Sustained-decline requirement
Smoothing alone isn't enough. A rule that fires the moment a single year dips below half the smoothed peak still gets fooled by ordinary noise. We tested this with a Monte Carlo simulation: take a citation rate that's genuinely flat, with no real decline at all, add negative-binomial noise (a more realistic stand-in for how overdispersed real citation counts are than plain Poisson noise), and see how often the single-year rule falsely calls a decline anyway. The answer: 71 to 89% of the time, more or less regardless of how many citations the paper gets per year.

Requiring the smoothed series to stay below half its peak for several years in a row, instead of just dipping below it once, cuts that false-positive rate substantially without giving up much ability to catch real declines (95% or better in every condition tested). Two things stand out in these numbers. Under low-noise, Poisson-like conditions, smoothing plus the sustained-decline requirement works well. Under the more realistic, heavier overdispersion (negative binomial, r=2), it only helps partway: even four years in a row still leaves the false-positive rate above 50%, no matter how many citations a paper gets per year. On the other hand, the cost to catching real declines is small and stays roughly the same, a few percentage points, whichever sustain-years setting you pick. Neither of these is hidden behind the default: the single-year reference result is always shown too, and this table is meant to be an honest picture of what "half-life reached" actually means under realistic noise, not a rosier one.

Smoothing and the sustained-decline rule fix two different problems, not the same one twice. Smoothing corrects a bad peak, an inflated single year. The sustained rule corrects a noisy decline test running against an otherwise-accurate peak. Fixing one doesn't fix the other: on Salter and Harris's raw, unsmoothed series, the inflated year-2000 peak still produces a 1-year half-life even under the strictest 4-year sustained setting, because the years right after 2000 genuinely do stay below half of that inflated number for four years running. Only smoothing the peak itself resolves that particular case.
Volume gate
A third guard, independent of the other two: if the smoothed peak comes in under 10 citations per year, there simply isn't enough signal for smoothing or the sustained-decline rule to work with. Both of those assume there's a real trend to average over. Below that floor, half-life isn't reported at all (each row of the peak/half-life table says so directly, rather than showing a number), and the aging sub-score is dropped from the composite entirely. The composite then becomes the weighted average of sustained rate and diffusion only, with those two weights renormalized to fill the gap. The scorecard always states plainly when this applies. It's never applied quietly.
Outlier detection
Separately from whichever smoothing basis is active, every year with more than 6 citations gets checked against its immediate neighbours. If its count is more than 2.5 times the average of the year before and the year after, it gets flagged as a possible indexing artifact. (Years right at the start or end of the series, with only one neighbour, aren't checked this way.) The 6-citation floor matters because an old or obscure paper's early history is often just single digits, and a jump from 1 citation to 5 clears that same 2.5x ratio just as easily as 79 does against a neighbourhood averaging 24, even though one is a real anomaly and the other is just noise. Without the floor, a wall of trivial flags would bury the genuine ones. This is only a diagnostic shown next to the half-life table. Flagged years aren't removed or changed anywhere, including in the CSV export.
What diffusion shares are counted against
The field shares shown, and the "1 minus top share" measure, are calculated against the live citing-works total described above. That means they won't add up to 100%, since some citing works don't have a field tag at all. The entropy-based measures (effective number of fields and its normalized version) and the Rao-Stirling measure work differently: they only count tagged citing works, because treating "effective number of fields" as a meaningful figure only makes sense if the shares add up to 1 in the first place. That's a deliberate choice, and the CSV export includes both the raw counts and every share calculated from them, so you can check the math either way.
Diffusion measures
Four measures get computed and shown for every paper: how much of the total the largest field takes up (inverted, so a higher number means the citing literature is more spread out), the raw number of distinct fields citing the paper, the effective number of fields (a Shannon-entropy-based figure that works like richness but downweights fields with only a handful of citations), and a Rao-Stirling-style diversity score that weights each citing field by how conceptually distant it is from the paper's own field, using the OpenAlex subject hierarchy: 0.33 for a shared parent field, 0.66 for only a shared domain, 1.0 otherwise. One of these four, selectable above and defaulting to effective number of fields, feeds into the composite score, divided by "max effective fields" and capped at 1.0. (The top-share and Rao-Stirling measures are already on a 0-to-1 scale.)
Field merging
OpenAlex's subfield tags are sometimes just wrong for this literature. Plenty of orthopaedic papers get tagged "Epidemiology" instead of "Surgery," for example. To correct for that, citing works tagged Epidemiology or Surgery are always relabeled to "Orthopedic Surgery (reclassified)" before diffusion is calculated. This isn't a setting you can turn off. It's a fixed correction for a known mistagging pattern, not an experimental option, so the composite score is always built on the corrected field breakdown. The raw, unmerged tags are still shown alongside on every scorecard so you can judge the reclassification yourself, and both versions are written into the CSV export.
Durability score
Sustained rate is the average annual citations over the last 5 complete years, divided by the peak annual citations on the active smoothing basis, capped at 1.0.
Aging is 1.0 if half-life was never reached (on the active smoothing basis and sustained-decline requirement), or half-life divided by the half-life reference otherwise, capped at 1.0. Unless the volume gate applies, in which case aging is dropped entirely and the composite becomes the weighted average of just the other two.
Diffusion is whichever measure you selected above, already on a 0-to-1 scale.
The composite score is 100 times the weighted average of whichever sub-scores are present. The default weights are equal thirds, renormalized over the remaining two when aging gets dropped. 70 or above is Durable, 40 to 69 is Moderately durable, below 40 is Faded.
Age gate
This overrides everything above. If a paper is under 10 years old, or its citation series (on the active smoothing basis) hasn't yet peaked and started declining, no score gets shown at all. Instead, the tool reports "Too recent to judge" and displays the components as background information only. That's on purpose: putting a number on a paper with no established citation history yet is exactly the kind of overclaim this project is arguing against.
What this cannot tell you
Whether a paper is actually correct, still clinically relevant, or something practitioners still teach from. Those questions are the two human-judgment fields on each scorecard. You fill them in yourself; the tool never computes them and never folds them into the composite.

Data comes from the OpenAlex API. Nothing you enter leaves your browser except the requests sent to OpenAlex, and nothing is stored anywhere else, because there's no server to store it on.
