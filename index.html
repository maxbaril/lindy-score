Lindy Durability Scorecard

Citation counts measure attention, not lasting value. This tool computes three bibliometric proxies for durability — sustained citation rate, citation half-life, and cross-specialty diffusion — from OpenAlex data, and leaves two further dimensions (educational reuse, expert-validated relevance) as explicit human-judgment fields, excluded from the score.

The composite score below is a provisional heuristic, not a validated instrument. It omits the two human-judgment dimensions. Papers under 10 years old, or whose citation series hasn't yet peaked and declined, are deliberately not scored — see how this is computed.

Peak / half-life smoothing basis

A single unusually high year (an indexing artifact, not a real citation spike) can register as an inflated peak immediately followed by a false "decline" in the raw series. Smoothing feeds both the half-life calculation and the sustained-rate sub-score's denominator so they rest on the same basis. The raw, unsmoothed half-life is always shown alongside the active basis on every scorecard — see also the outlier flags below each chart, which name the specific years driving this.

Sustained-decline requirement for half-life

Half-life is "reached" at the first later year that begins a run of this many consecutive years all at or below half the (smoothed) peak, rather than any single year dipping below it once. Monte Carlo simulation under negative-binomial citation noise found the single-year rule flags a spurious half-life on a genuinely flat, non-declining series roughly 71–89% of the time; requiring several consecutive years reduces this substantially without materially reducing detection of real declines (>=95% in the same simulation across every condition tested) — see how this is computed for the full comparison. The original single-year result on the raw series is always shown for reference regardless of this setting.

Field merging

OpenAlex subfield tags are sometimes substantively wrong for this literature — e.g. many orthopaedic papers get tagged "Epidemiology" rather than "Surgery." Merge rules are a user reclassification, not source data, and are always shown alongside the untouched raw tags.

Rare-field noise threshold

A field must fail both the citing-works and the percent test to survive the join — i.e. it's dropped if it's under the works threshold OR under the percent threshold. Richness and the entropy-based measures are far more sensitive to this than the top-share measure; compare the scorecard's raw/merged diffusion table with the toggle on and off.

How this is computed, and what it cannot tell you

Data source
OpenAlex REST API, no authentication, queried live in your browser. The annual citation series is built from works?filter=cites:{id}&group_by=publication_year — every citing work grouped by its own publication year. This is the same query OpenAlex's own web interface uses for its Year facet, and it returns complete history with no truncation. Field breakdown comes from the analogous works?filter=cites:{id}&group_by=primary_topic.subfield.id. Both group_by calls request the maximum 200 groups per page; a citation-year span or field count exceeding 200 is flagged defensively, though no real paper approaches that. Citing works with no recorded publication year are reported separately and excluded from the annual series, not silently dropped.
Two citation totals, shown side by side
This tool deliberately avoids the Work object's precomputed counts_by_year field: it's a periodically-refreshed shortcut capped at roughly the last decade, which is why an earlier version of this tool showed a truncated series and a suspiciously recent "peak year" for older papers. Instead, every annual count and the total citing-works figure are derived directly from the cites: filter above. That live total can differ slightly from the Work object's own cached cited_by_count — OpenAlex documents that the two are refreshed on different schedules — so both are shown on each scorecard rather than treating either as the "wrong" one. All calculations here use the live cites: filter total.
Series-start check
The tool checks that the earliest citing work is reasonably close to the paper's own publication year (within 5 years) and flags it on the scorecard if not — this can indicate an indexing gap rather than a genuinely slow-to-be-cited paper. Since the series is now the complete history rather than a capped recent window, this is a plausibility check, not a truncation warning.
Citation persistence
Sum of citations over the last five complete calendar years (the current, still-in-progress year is excluded). "Still rising" means the most recent complete year is at or above every other year in the returned series.
Citation half-life
Years from the peak annual-citation year to the first later year that begins a run of several consecutive years all at or below half the peak value (see "sustained-decline requirement" below). If no such run ever occurs, this is reported as "not reached after N years" rather than assigning an arbitrary number.

Smoothing basis. A single unusually high year — an indexing artifact rather than a real citation spike — can register as an inflated "peak." (Concretely: Salter & Harris 1963 shows 1999=17, 2000=79, 2001=31 in an otherwise smooth series — reading 79 as the peak overstates it by roughly 3×, against neighbouring years averaging 24.) The default basis is therefore a 3-year centered moving average: each year's smoothed value is the mean of itself and its immediate calendar neighbours (at the start/end of the series, whatever window is available, rather than dropping those years). Peak and half-life are identified on this smoothed curve, and the smoothed peak — not the raw one — is also the denominator for the sustained-rate sub-score below, so both metrics rest on the same basis. You can switch the basis to raw (unsmoothed) or a 5-year average above; the raw, unsmoothed half-life under the original single-year rule is always shown alongside the active basis on every scorecard, never hidden in favor of the smoothed one.
Sustained-decline requirement
Smoothing the series is not enough on its own. A single-year rule — "the first later year at or below half the (smoothed) peak" — still gets fooled by ordinary noise: on a Monte Carlo simulation of a genuinely flat citation rate (no real decline at all) under negative-binomial noise, which is a better model of real citation-count overdispersion than Poisson, the single-year rule reported a spurious half-life 71–89% of the time, largely independent of citation volume. Requiring the smoothed series to stay at or below half its peak for several consecutive years — rather than dipping below it once — cuts this substantially without giving up real-decline detection, which stayed ≥95% in every condition tested. Two things worth reading plainly from these tables. First, smoothing plus a sustained-decline requirement helps a lot under low-noise (Poisson-like) conditions but only partially under the stronger, more realistic overdispersion (negative binomial, r=2) — even 4 consecutive years still leaves false-positive rates above 50% there, at every citation volume tested. Second, the cost in real-decline detection is small and roughly uniform (a few percentage points) regardless of which sustain-years value is chosen. Neither of these facts is hidden by the default: the reference single-year result is always shown alongside, and this table is the honest picture of what "half-life reached" does and doesn't mean under realistic noise.

Smoothing and the sustained-decline rule are complementary, not redundant: smoothing corrects a bad peak (an inflated single year), while the sustained rule corrects a noisy decline test against an otherwise-accurate peak. Neither fixes the other's failure mode — on Salter & Harris 1963's raw, unsmoothed series, the inflated 2000 peak still reads as a 1-year half-life even at the strictest 4-year sustained setting, because the years immediately after 2000 are genuinely, consecutively below half of that inflated number. Only smoothing the peak itself resolves that case.
Volume gate
A third, independent guard: below a smoothed peak of 10 citations per year (on the active smoothing basis), there is too little citation volume for smoothing or the sustained-decline rule to meaningfully fix — both assume enough signal to average over. Below that floor, half-life is not reported (shown as "not reported" rather than a number, on every basis in the peak/half-life table independently) and the aging sub-score is dropped from the composite, which is then the weighted mean of sustained rate and diffusion alone, renormalized over their two weights. This is shown explicitly on the scorecard whenever it applies — never silently.
Outlier detection
Independent of which smoothing basis is active, every year with more than 6 raw citations is checked against its own immediate neighbours: it's flagged as a possible indexing artifact if its raw count exceeds 2.5× the mean of the year before and the year after (edge years, with only one neighbour, aren't checked). The 6-citation floor exists because an old or obscure paper's sparse early history is mostly single digits, where a jump from 1 citation to 5 clears the same 2.5× ratio as 79 against a peer group of 24 — real in relative terms, meaningless in absolute ones — and would otherwise bury a genuine artifact under a wall of trivial flags. This is a diagnostic shown alongside the half-life table, not a silent data correction — flagged years are not removed or altered anywhere in the underlying series or the CSV export.
Cross-specialty diffusion — denominator
Displayed field shares and the "1 − top share" measure use the live citing-works total (the same cites: filter total described above) as the denominator, per spec — they will not sum to 100% since some citing works are untagged. The entropy-based measures (effective number of fields, its normalized form) and the Rao-Stirling measure instead renormalize shares among tagged citing works only, because exp(Shannon entropy) is only a meaningful "effective category count" over a distribution that sums to 1. This denominator split is a deliberate, documented choice — see the CSV export for both the raw counts and every derived share.
Diffusion measures
Four are computed and shown for every paper: (a) 1 − largest field's share, (b) field richness (raw distinct-field count), (c) effective number of fields = exp(Shannon entropy), plus its normalized form H / ln(S), and (d) a Rao-Stirling-style distance-weighted diversity anchored at the paper's own primary subfield, where citing fields sharing a parent field score distance 0.33, sharing only a domain score 0.66, and otherwise score 1.0. One measure — selectable above, default (c) — feeds the composite as min(measure / max-effective-fields, 1.0), except (a) and (d) which are already 0–1.
Field merging
Off by default. When enabled, user-defined rules combine two or more raw OpenAlex subfield tags into one relabeled field before diffusion is recomputed. Raw and merged shares are always shown side by side, never merged alone, and the rules are written into the CSV export.
Durability score
Sustained rate = mean annual citations over the last 5 complete years ÷ peak annual citations on the active smoothing basis, capped at 1.0.
Aging = 1.0 if half-life (active smoothing basis, active sustained-decline requirement) was never reached; otherwise min(half-life years ÷ half-life reference, 1.0) — or dropped entirely if the volume gate applies (see above), in which case the composite is the weighted mean of sustained rate and diffusion only.
Diffusion = the selected measure above, as a 0–1 sub-score.
Composite = 100 × weighted mean of whichever sub-scores are present (default weights 1/3 each, renormalized over the two remaining when aging is dropped). Bands: ≥70 Durable, 40–69 Moderately durable, <40 Faded.
Age gate
Overrides the bands. If the paper is under 10 years old, or its annual series (on the active smoothing basis) has not yet peaked and declined, no score is shown — the tool reports "Too recent to judge" and displays the components as provisional context. This is deliberate: scoring a paper with no established citation trajectory yet is the overclaim this project argues against.
What this cannot tell you
Whether a paper is correct, whether it's still clinically relevant, or whether practitioners actually still teach from it. Those are the two human-judgment fields on each scorecard, entered by you, never computed, and never included in the composite.
